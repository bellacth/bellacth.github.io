Behind the essay

What are we trying to protect?

These notes hold the questions that sit beneath the story. They are not a second argument hidden behind the first. They are a record of the philosophical traditions, tensions, and research that shaped it.

01

Humanism after human superiority

The word humanism can point in several directions. Liberal humanism begins with the dignity and moral equality of persons. It asks institutions to treat people as ends rather than merely as instruments, and it grounds rights in something more stable than intelligence, productivity, or social rank. This is the tradition closest to the claim that a person should not need to outperform a machine in order to matter.7

Existential and phenomenological approaches begin somewhere more intimate. They emphasize embodiment, mortality, vulnerability, projects, and care. From this view, intelligence is not simply the production of correct answers. Human understanding occurs inside a life that can be wounded, obligated, surprised, and changed. The important question is therefore not only whether AI can perform a task, but what performing the task does to the person.

Critical posthumanism complicates both traditions. It warns that the ideal “human” celebrated by Western thought has often been independent, rational, able-bodied, male, and dominant over animals and nature. Many actual people were excluded from full humanity by failing to match that ideal. Protecting humans from AI cannot mean rebuilding the same hierarchy with machines placed at the bottom.8

A durable humanism might therefore make two claims at once. Human beings have dignity that does not depend on comparative ability. Human beings are also relational creatures whose identity has never been sealed off from tools, environments, other species, and one another. This is a humanism without a throne.

02

Work and purpose are not the same question

When people ask what humans will do if AI performs more work, they may be asking at least five different questions: How will people receive income? How will they structure time? Where will they encounter others? How will they contribute? How will they know that they are good at something?

No single policy answers all five. A basic income can protect survival and bargaining power without creating community. A job guarantee can offer structure and recognition while preserving unnecessary labor. Shared ownership can redistribute power without telling anyone how to spend a Tuesday afternoon. Shorter working weeks can distribute leisure unevenly if care remains unsupported.

This is why disagreement about post-work policy often hides disagreement about the meaning of work itself. Susskind argues that policymakers choosing between income replacement and job guarantees rely on assumptions about whether work provides a form of meaning that cannot be replaced.4 The more careful answer may be that work is meaningful for some people, under some conditions, while still being an unnecessarily harsh gatekeeper for income and dignity.

Replacing wages is an economic problem. Replacing the social world built around wages is a cultural one.

03

Does anything need to remain uniquely human?

There are several ways to answer this question. We could search for one remaining ability, perhaps consciousness, creativity, moral judgment, embodiment, or love. This would preserve a clear boundary, but it would also make our understanding of human value depend on a factual claim that future technology might unsettle.

We could integrate more closely with AI. From this perspective, human beings do not compete with the tool because the person and the tool become part of the same thinking system. This might expand what people can do, but it would also raise questions about unequal access, dependence, and who controls the systems through which we think.

We could focus on embodiment and mortality. A human life takes place inside one vulnerable body and eventually ends. Meaning may come partly from the fact that our attention, energy, and time are limited. Or we could decide that human beings do not need to remain unique in order to remain valuable. This may be the most difficult answer because it removes the competition entirely. We already claim that greater intelligence does not give one human being greater dignity than another. AI may reveal whether we ever truly believed that.

The possibility of machine consciousness makes the question harder. Current research does not establish consciousness in existing AI systems, while also finding no obvious reason to declare conscious artificial systems impossible in principle.9 If moral status one day expands, human dignity will need to coexist with duties toward new kinds of beings.

04

What might be worth preserving

Not all difficulty is meaningful. Some difficulty is simply exclusion wearing the language of rigor. But certain forms of effort develop capacities that a person may later need: attention, patience, memory, judgment, and the ability to remain with confusion. The goal is not to preserve unaided work because it is somehow purer. It is to distinguish between assistance that leaves a person more capable and assistance that only leaves the final product more polished.

Responsibility may also be worth preserving. A human signature at the end of an automated decision means very little if the person signing lacks the time, knowledge, or authority to disagree. Human oversight should preserve answerability rather than merely provide someone to blame. Particular relationships matter too. Care is not valuable only because a need has been met efficiently. Who arrives, who remembers, and who continues to return can be part of the need itself.

Finally, people may need the ability to refuse. A choice to use AI is meaningful only if someone can also decline it without losing access to education, employment, healthcare, or public life. The right to use a powerful tool and the right not to use it may need to develop together. Feminist, decolonial, and disability perspectives add another question: who defines the problem, whose labor becomes invisible, and which forms of dependence are treated as failure?1011

AI can also appear weightless while depending on electricity, water, minerals, chips, land, and labor. The IEA describes both the rising energy demands of data centers and the possibility that AI improves energy systems.12 Any account of human flourishing that ignores those costs simply places them outside the frame.

05

Why the essay ends in scenarios

A forecast asks what will happen. A scenario asks what would follow if several uncertain conditions became true together. This distinction matters because technical capability does not determine one social order. The same system can support public abundance, private rent extraction, democratic participation, or surveillance depending on ownership, law, energy, labor power, and the capacity to refuse.

The six endings are therefore not personality types and they are not predictions. Each gathers a set of values into a coherent institution. Each protects something important and creates a characteristic danger. The Civic Abundance Society may turn belonging into obligation. The Open Horizon may turn freedom into isolation. The Protected Human Economy may preserve empty work. Supported Independence may secure life without creating purpose. The Accountable Partnership may reduce oversight to ceremony. The Negotiated Middle may become too slow to resist concentrated power.

Scenario practice is most useful when it preserves uncertainty rather than disguising a preferred future as inevitable. Futures scholarship often uses contrasting archetypes to reveal assumptions, not to select a winner.13 The interaction asks the reader to notice what they protect first when no option protects everything.
