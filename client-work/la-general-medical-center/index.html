Los Angeles General Medical Center

Pro-Bono Consulting Project ManagerSep’25 — Dec’25

View full PDF ↗

Led a team of 25+ consultants to develop a patient-centric wayfinding system for one of the largest public hospitals in Los Angeles

At a
glance

Los Angeles General Medical Center asked our team to redesign its wayfinding system across a complex, multi-building campus serving a diverse patient population. The project addressed navigation challenges that disproportionately affected first-time visitors, non-English speakers, elderly patients, and individuals with disabilities. By mapping the full patient journey from pre-arrival to appointment, we identified breakdowns in communication, signage, and digital tools. Our recommendations centered on a hybrid wayfinding model combining multilingual pre-arrival communication, ADA-compliant physical signage, and digital mapping solutions to reduce confusion, improve punctuality, and enhance overall patient experience without requiring major structural changes.

01 / Context

The Problem

Los Angeles General Medical Center is one of the largest public hospitals in Los Angeles, serving a large and diverse patient population across a complex, multi-building campus.

Finding your way through a hospital starts before walking through the doors: figuring out where your appointment is, which building to enter, where to park, and what information to follow once you arrive. Those challenges become even more significant for first-time visitors, non-English speakers, elderly patients, and people with disabilities. Our goal was to understand that entire experience and make that entire journey more intuitive, inclusive, and efficient, while prioritizing solutions that could work within LA General's existing infrastructure rather than requiring major structural changes.

02 / Research

The Approach

We started by treating wayfinding as an end-to-end patient journey: Pre-Arrival → Arrival → Outdoor Wayfinding → Indoor Wayfinding.

Through background research and an on-site assessment, we identified friction across each stage as shown below:

01

Pre-arrival

Appointment emails only had room details, lacked image-driven visual instructions, and sometimes contained outdated information.

02

Arrival

Parking assignments and locations were unclear, valet was underused, and public transit drop-off instructions were difficult to follow.

03

Outdoor wayfinding

Signs were difficult to read, building labels were inconsistent, and connected hallways were not clearly indicated.

04

Indoor wayfinding

Maps and floor numbers were inconsistent, digital directories conflicted, and patients relied heavily on staff for directions.

Accessibility added another layer of consideration. We had to consider how the same environment could be experienced differently by first-time visitors, multilingual patients, elderly patients, and people with hearing, cognitive, or mobility impairments.

◖

Hearing

Pair auditory information with visible equivalents and consistent hearing-assist symbols.

◌

Cognitive

Minimize decision points, jargon, and inconsistent department naming.

↗

Mobility

Design for different lines of sight, clear routes, non-glare finishes, and tactile signage.

03 / System

Designing the Patient Journey

Our recommendation centered on a hybrid physical and digital wayfinding system that could support patients throughout their journey.

Before arrival

Communicate in context

Redesigned appointment confirmations, visual instructions, multiple languages, and consistent third-party map information.

On campus

Make decisions legible

Clear parking signs and high-contrast directional signage at the points where drivers and pedestrians actually choose a route.

Inside

Connect physical + digital

Standardized directories, color cues, updated floor maps, and QR-supported navigation that reinforce one another.

04 / Execution

Making the Recommendations Implementable

One of the most important parts of the project was pushing beyond “what should LA General do?” to “what would it actually take to do it?”

Our final deliverable included sample communication materials and signage designs, recommended signage locations, digital directory concepts, accessibility considerations, technology comparisons, implementation timelines, staff training considerations, long-term maintenance processes, and cost estimates.

We also thought about what happens after implementation. For example, we proposed processes for keeping physical and digital maps current as the campus changes, rather than treating wayfinding as a one-time redesign.

The goal was to leave the client with more than a vision. We wanted to give them a path from recommendation to execution.

01

Placement

Recommended signage locations and decision points across the campus.

02

Prototype

03

Adoption

Staff training, accessibility considerations, timelines, and cost estimates.

04

Maintenance

Ownership processes for keeping physical and digital maps current as the campus changes.

05 / Outcome

The Impact

Our final recommendation brought together communication, physical signage, accessibility, digital navigation, and long-term maintenance into one patient-centered wayfinding system. The client responded extremely positively to the work and ultimately asked us to present our recommendations with LA General's CEO in attendance.

06 / Reflection

My Takeaways

This project taught me two things.

First

First, a recommendation is only as useful as its ability to be implemented. Some of our strongest work came after we had already identified the answer—when we had to think through where something would go, what it would look like, who would maintain it, what it would cost, and whether it would actually work for the people using it.

Second

Second, it changed how I think about leadership. Leading 25+ people was initially overwhelming. I learned quickly that assigning work wasn't enough; people did their best work when they understood why their contribution mattered and had ownership over a part of the problem that interested them. I began organizing teams around people's strengths and development goals rather than treating everyone as interchangeable capacity.

Interestingly, the two lessons came back to the same principle: we couldn't design wayfinding by assuming every patient navigated the hospital the same way, and I couldn't lead a team by assuming every person was motivated in the same way. So whether designing a system or leading a team, I learned to start with the people who actually have to use it.
